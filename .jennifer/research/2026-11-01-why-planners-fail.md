# Facet B: Why personal productivity, habit and self-tracking tools help or fail (HCI and behavioral evidence)

Date: 2026-11-01. Scope excludes what `2026-10-23-productivity-methods-evidence.md` and `2026-10-23-personal-tracking-systems.md` already cover (implementation intentions, self-monitoring meta, goal setting, planning fallacy, attention residue, breaks, Lally, TimeAware, leisure scheduling, Epstein 2015 lived informatics).

Grades: **A** = meta-analysis or RCT, **B** = single peer-reviewed study or large reputable survey, **C** = practitioner, vendor or book, **D** = anecdote. "(abstract)" means I verified only the abstract. "(secondary)" means the figure comes from a summary, not the paper.

## 2. Why tools fail (ranked by how strong and how general the evidence is)

1. **Upkeep costs more than the tool gives back.** Lazar: participants abandoned about 80% of devices within 2 months, citing upkeep, useless data and poor fit with self-image (#14). Haraty: people find dedicated apps inflexible, slow to learn, and "over-organizing", so they go back to paper or a Word document (#4, #5). Meyer: a self-report that takes several minutes gets disabled, and one that takes seconds gets answered (#19). This failure mode has the most consistent support across sources.
2. **Capture is slower than a scrap.** To-dos live in about 11 places, and only 14% sit in a list (#2). Scraps win because they are fast to capture and flexible (#6). A planner that is not the fastest place to write something down becomes one place among eleven.
3. **Broken streaks and visible failure lower engagement.** Showing a broken streak reduces later behavior, especially when people blame themselves (#10). The "what-the-hell" effect has weaker support (#13). Low self-compassion goes with stress and procrastination (#23, #24). Earlier repo research found half of lapsers feel guilty (Epstein 2015).
4. **Reminders take the place of habit formation.** Reminders produce repetition without automaticity, and the behavior depends on the app (#8, #9). Habit features that are only "tick + streak + push" reproduce exactly what Stawarz criticized.
5. **Data without meaning or action.** Data perceived as useless leads to abandonment (#14). Insights need to be actionable (#20). Reflection that only notices numbers is shallow, and repeated prompts get boring (#21). Only 28% looked at the retrospective view daily (#19).
6. **Failures cascade from collection.** A gap in collection breaks reflection and action downstream (#17). Fully automatic collection removes awareness (#18).
7. **Too many options and settings.** Choice overload appears when options are complex and preferences are uncertain (#28), which describes a new user facing an empty planner with many settings. People mostly keep whatever default they are given (#27).
8. **Tasks rot.** Tasks that are not done within 2 weeks rarely get done (#3). Lists grow without bound unless something forces a decision.

## 3. What keeps people using them

- **Routine, curiosity, usefulness and hope** (#14). Of these, routine is the one the product can influence.
- **Interactions that take seconds**, such as one-tap self-reports and quick capture (#6, #19).
- **Personal fit.** People do best with tools they can bend to their own rules: DIYers "talked enthusiastically about their PTM systems" (#4), and needs keep changing (#5).
- **Novel, short, dialogue-like reflection.** 16/33 people kept using the reflection system without pay (#21). Emotion-centric start and end-of-day dialogues helped people detach and reattach (#22).
- **A weekly cadence for looking back.** Meyer's pilot users asked for weekly summaries, and most people did not look at retrospection daily (#19). This is consistent with earlier repo findings.
- **Visible progress.** Seeing progress motivates (#25, grade C only). An intact streak keeps engagement up (#10). Slack built into goals helps people keep going after a miss (#11).
- **Social expectation.** Tasks someone else is waiting on get done (77% vs 53%), and so do meetings (#1).

## 4. Design implications for skkil/time (concrete and testable)

1. **Make quick add the fastest place to capture anything.** The hotkey to saved task should take under 2 seconds, with no required fields and everything landing in the Inbox. *Test:* median time from global hotkey to saved task, and the share of tasks created through quick add rather than forms. (#2, #6)
2. **Let tasks age out.** Flag Today or Inbox tasks that have been carried over for more than 14 days, and give one-click choices in the weekly review: do / schedule / drop / someday. *Test:* the size of the open-task backlog over 8 weeks with and without the prompt. (#3)
3. **Habits: replace streak counters with a forgiving consistency display.** Show "X of last 14 days" or the weekly hit rate. Offer emergency reserves, e.g. 1–2 skip days a week that the user sets in advance and that are counted, not hidden. If a streak is shown at all, a missed day should be repairable and the copy should not blame the user. *Test:* A/B the next-week completion rate after a first miss, streak vs reserves. (#10, #11, #13)
4. **Require an anchor, not just a reminder, for habits.** The habit form should ask "After I ___" (an existing routine), and push reminders should stay off by default. *Test:* self-report automaticity (SRBAI) at 4 weeks for anchored vs reminder-only habits. Do not ship more reminder types. (#8, #9)
5. **Keep the daily check-in to one tap.** It is already optional, so keep it a single tap plus an optional line, and never make it a modal. A daily prompt that takes more than about 10 seconds will be skipped or disabled. *Test:* check-in completion rate and the rate at which it gets disabled. (#19)
6. **Rotate the wording of review prompts.** Keep a pool of reflection prompts and add at least one about feelings ("What drained / energized you this week?"), not only task counts. *Test:* the weekly review completion rate over 8+ weeks, comparing a fixed prompt set with a rotating one. (#21, #22)
7. **End every review insight with one suggested action.** For example: "You planned 22h, tracked 14h → plan 3 fewer blocks next week?" Pure charts are not enough. (#20)
8. **Fill in tracked time automatically and confirm it by hand.** Pre-fill time entries from schedule blocks or ActivityWatch, and have the user confirm or correct them at the end of the day. This keeps awareness (#18) without the cost of manual entry (#14). *Test:* the share of days with any tracked time, comparing a manual timer with suggest-and-confirm.
9. **Make re-entry after a lapse feel normal.** After 3 or more missed days, show a "welcome back" summary with no red counters, plus a one-click "archive stale / reschedule" action. Copy should be self-compassionate ("Missing days is normal; pick one thing for today"). *Test:* the return rate within 7 days after a gap of 7 or more days. (#23, #24, #10)
10. **Ship good defaults and hide most settings.** Use one default view, a soft cap of 3 outcomes, and a short Today list. Advanced options go behind "More". *Test:* how many sessions a new user needs before the first planned day, with full settings vs minimal settings. (#27, #28, #4)
11. **Show progress in the planner, not only in reports.** Today should show completions and progress on the week's outcomes ("2 of 3 outcomes moving") rather than the remaining backlog. (#25, #10)
12. **Treat focus blocks as rearranging work, not adding to it.** Report focus blocks as "protected X h" and do not promise more output. (#29)
13. **Keep notifications silent by default.** Leave in-app notices on but OS push off unless the user opts in, with one optional end-of-day nudge. (#26, #8)
14. **Make "stop using it" a supported state.** Allow pausing or retiring a habit with a summary ("you ran this 9 weeks"), and do not count it as failure. (#15, #16)

## 5. Gaps

- **I found no peer-reviewed study of to-do *app* abandonment rates** or of time-blocking tools in general. The focus-time evidence comes from Microsoft only.
- **Streak freezes and streak repair:** the only evidence is Silverman & Barasch's attenuation finding plus Duolingo vendor numbers. No independent trial was found.
- **Emergency reserves:** a single field experiment in a manuscript version. I did not confirm whether it was published, and one student replication failed.
- **What-the-hell effect:** I could not open the primary chapter. A Tomiyama 2009 challenge is mentioned only in summaries.
- **Not verified:** the full results of Epstein 2016 (PMC blocked), Clawson 2015 (categories not read), Kersten-van Dijk 2017 critical review (abstract only), and Baumer 2014 "Reviewing reflection" (no content retrieved).
- **Stawarz 2015:** I did not verify the sample size or effect sizes.
- **Product guilt or shame caused by productivity dashboards** has no direct study. The evidence is indirect (#10, #23, #24) plus TimeAware in the earlier repo research.
- **Onboarding complexity in planners specifically:** only general choice-overload and default evidence was found.
- **Amabile's progress principle:** only book-level and secondary sources. I found no independent replication.
- **Wohl et al. 2010:** n differs between secondary sources (119 vs 134). I did not open the original.
